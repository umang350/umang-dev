## Why This Belongs in a PM Portfolio

Most PM portfolios only show work product: PRDs, roadmaps, case studies from jobs. This project is different — it's a product I built from zero with no team, no budget, and no brand, using the same skills I apply at work.

It also gave me a real-time data environment to experiment in, with direct user feedback (comments, retention curves, subscriber behavior) that most corporate products don't provide. I learned things here that I brought back into my day job.

## The Product

A YouTube channel for developers interested in tech careers in Japan and Asia. The audience: software engineers, CS students, and career changers thinking about working abroad in tech.

The initial thesis: there's a niche of English-speaking developers interested in the Japan tech scene, but content serving them is either too surface-level (travel vlogs) or too narrow (purely Japanese-language resources). A channel that bridges the gap — technical, career-focused, bilingual — could own that niche.

That thesis turned out to be mostly right.

## How I Ran It Like a Product

**Content pillars as product strategy.** Before recording anything, I defined 3 content pillars: (1) career navigation in Japan tech, (2) technical deep-dives relevant to the Japan market, (3) personal documentation of the SDE → PM transition. Each video fit into one pillar. This made it easier to build a recognizable brand and measure which pillars drove subscribers vs. watch time.

**Retention curves as the primary metric.** YouTube provides per-video audience retention data — a curve showing where viewers drop off. I treated every video's retention curve as a product insight. When I noticed 40% of viewers dropped at the 3-minute mark consistently, I restructured my intro format. When long-form videos had higher average view duration than shorts, I doubled down on long-form despite shorts getting more initial impressions.

**Format experiments as A/B tests.** I ran structured experiments: same topic, different hook style; same content, different thumbnail text. The sample sizes were too small for statistical significance, but the directional signals were consistent enough to inform decisions.

## What Worked

- **Niche clarity** drove organic growth without paid promotion
- **Consistency** (weekly cadence for 18 months) compounded over time — new videos discovered through old ones
- **Community engagement** in comments surfaced content ideas I wouldn't have thought of; the audience was effectively doing user research for me

## What Didn't Work

- **Chasing algorithm trends** (shorts, trending topics) consistently underperformed niche-focused content. The algorithm rewards watch time and retention, not novelty.
- **Production quality** had diminishing returns beyond a baseline. Upgrading microphone quality helped; upgrading lighting beyond "decent" didn't move metrics.

## What I Brought Back to Work

Running a channel end-to-end — strategy, production, distribution, community — gave me a healthy respect for the full product lifecycle. The most useful thing: direct user feedback at scale. Comments are unfiltered, immediate, and honest in a way that enterprise user research rarely is. I now push hard for more direct user access in my PM work because I know what you learn when you actually talk to users.
