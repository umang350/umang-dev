## The Problem

Warehouse planners were switching between three separate tools to do a single workflow: check inventory, run a forecast, and submit an allocation order. Each tool had its own login, its own data model, and its own way of representing stock levels — which didn't always agree with each other.

The tools were aging. Maintenance was expensive. But more importantly, planners had worked around the tools for so long that nobody had a clear picture of what the actual workflow *should* look like.

## My Role

This was my first full PM role after transitioning from engineering. I owned the product vision for the migration: from problem definition through launch, iteration, and handoff to a dedicated PM successor.

The transition from engineer to PM forced a specific discipline: I had to stop proposing solutions before I understood problems. It took about three months to fully internalize that.

## Discovery

I ran structured interviews with 14 warehouse managers and planners across 3 facilities over 4 weeks. The finding that changed the product direction: planners didn't primarily need better data — they needed to trust the data they already had. The three-tool fragmentation had made them skeptical of anything the system told them, so they'd built their own manual verification steps (spreadsheets, phone calls) on top of it.

Migrating to a unified platform wasn't enough. We also had to earn back trust in the data.

## Key Decisions

**Migration strategy — big-bang vs. incremental**

Engineering pushed for a clean migration: shut down the old tools on a date, migrate data, go live on the new platform. I pushed back. My concern: if planners had already built manual workarounds, a forced cutover would surface every undocumented edge case at once, with no escape valve.

We ran a 6-week parallel period where the new platform and legacy tools ran simultaneously. Planners could verify the new platform's output against the old tools. Trust built gradually. The cutover, when it came, was uneventful.

**Prioritization — feature requests vs. trust-building**

The backlog had 40+ items. The top requests from management were new features (better reporting, new allocation rules). The top need from planners was data consistency and auditability. I chose planners' needs. This was a hard conversation with stakeholders who wanted to see new capability.

The payoff: NPS went from 32 (at migration kickoff) to 71 (six months post-launch). Feature requests were easier to justify and fund once trust was established.

## Impact

- Migrated 3 legacy tools into a unified platform, eliminating context-switching for planners
- Introduced data-driven prioritization using usage analytics and NPS surveys — a practice that stuck after I moved on
- Shipped demand forecasting feature that improved seasonal stock planning accuracy

## What I Learned

The biggest misconception I had coming from engineering: that writing a good PRD is the hard part. It isn't. The hard part is knowing what to write the PRD *about*. Discovery isn't a phase you do before product work — it's continuous, and it's what separates PMs who ship features from PMs who solve problems.
